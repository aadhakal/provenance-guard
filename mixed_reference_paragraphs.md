The arrival of the American system of measurement caused confusion and conflict. The American system relied on a square grid for the division of land, while the Spanish used irregular distributions of land that often corresponded with social and political values. For example, closer to a seaport, or for a person of high rank, a vara could reflect the landholder’s importance by being slightly longer. The Americans wanted their property demarcated equally, inch by inch. A disagreement over a unit could therefore become a disagreement over a fence, a field, or access to a road. Two neighbours might both believe their measurements were right while drawing the boundary in different places. Writing down the number was only part of the job. They also needed to agree on the length that number represented before the survey could settle anything.

The water cycle describes how water evaporates from the surface of the earth, rises into the atmosphere, cools and condenses into rain or snow in clouds, and falls again to the surface as precipitation. A puddle drying after a storm is one small part of that movement. The water disappears from view without leaving the wider system. The cycling of water in and out of the atmosphere is a significant aspect of the weather patterns on Earth. Looking at only the rain misses most of the journey. Some water returns to the air quickly, while some takes a much longer route through the ground.
